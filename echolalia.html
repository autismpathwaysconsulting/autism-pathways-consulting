When your child keeps repeating words and phrases

By CJ LimAutism Educator & Founder of APC

Reviewed 17 September 2026

Your child repeats a line from a cartoon or a question you have just asked. You may wonder what it means and whether to answer. Repetition can tell you something worth exploring, but it cannot on its own show how much your child understands.

In this guide
  • What echolalia can mean
  • Respond to the child and the situation
  • Try, notice and adjust
  • When to seek more support

What echolalia can mean

Echolalia is repetition of speech heard from another person or source, immediately or later. It may serve communication, participation, enjoyment or self-regulation. The purpose can vary across moments, and sometimes it is not clear. Avoid assuming every repeated phrase has a hidden message or follows a single language-development pathway.

Be curious about context. Neither fluent repetition nor limited original speech is a reliable measure of understanding by itself.

Respond to the child and the situation

1

Notice when the phrase appears

Record the words and what was happening around them. A line associated with a favourite show could be an invitation to share it, a familiar sound or something else. One observation is not enough to decide.

2

Offer a response they can correct

If “Ready, steady, go” appears beside the door, you might ask or show “Outside?” Give your child a way to confirm, refuse or choose another option. Respond warmly even when you are unsure of the meaning.

3

Avoid making silence the goal

Repetition that is enjoyable or helpful does not automatically need changing. If volume or timing creates a practical difficulty, consider the environment and support options without shaming your child or removing their available communication.

4

Add accessible ways to communicate

Model a useful short message, gesture or symbol in the relevant situation without demanding repetition. Keep existing AAC available. A speech and language therapist can help assess understanding and communication across contexts.

When repetition is tiring for you

It is possible to respect your child's communication and still need a break. If safe supervision is available, share care for a while or move to a quieter nearby space. You do not have to answer every repetition identically. Notice whether your child is asking for a response, and explain a pause in a way they understand.

Try, notice and adjust

Choose one recurring phrase and note several examples, including your response and what followed. You may find a useful pattern, or you may remain unsure. Bring those examples to a speech and language appointment instead of treating the pattern as a diagnosis.

When to seek more support

Seek speech and language support if you are concerned about comprehension, development or your child's ability to express needs. Ask for goals that improve meaningful communication and participation. Repetition does not need to disappear for communication to be valuable.
